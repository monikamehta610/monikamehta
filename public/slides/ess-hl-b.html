HL.b.1

Supply and Demand

Key UnderstandingEconomics studies how humans produce, distribute and consume goods and services, both individually and collectively.

At the core of conventional economics is the relationship between Supply (how much of a resource is available) and Demand (how much consumers want to buy it).

  • Market Equilibrium: Established where the supply curve and demand curve intersect. The price producers are willing to accept equals the price consumers are willing to pay.
  • Shifting the Demand Curve: Can be caused by changes in consumer income, changes in preferences (e.g. going vegan), or the price of substitute goods.
  • Government Subsidies: If the government subsidizes renewable energy, it lowers the cost of production, shifting the supply curve to the right (more is supplied at a lower price).
HL.b.2

Environmental Economics

Key UnderstandingEnvironmental economics is economics applied to the environment and environmental issues.

Economists play a massive role in managing environmental resources by applying economic principles to pollution and conservation. They attempt to put a financial value on nature to protect it.

Market-Based Instruments

Used to address pollution by changing financial incentives:

  • Carbon Taxes
  • Cap and Trade (Tradable Permits)
  • Subsidies for Green Tech
  • Fines for illegal dumping

Technocentric vs Ecocentric

  • Technocentric: Nature is a resource to be managed for human benefit. Growth is good.
  • Ecocentric: Nature has intrinsic value. We must limit economic growth to protect it.
HL.b.3 / HL.b.4

Market Failure & Polluter Pays Principle

HL.b.3 Market failure occurs when the allocation of goods and services by the free market imposes negative impacts on the environment.

Market failure often happens due to Negative Externalities (a cost suffered by a third party as a result of an economic transaction, e.g. a factory polluting a river and residents getting sick). The product's price doesn't reflect its true cost to society.

HL.b.4 When the market fails to prevent negative impacts, the polluter-pays principle may be applied.
  • Polluter Pays: The party responsible for producing pollution is held responsible for paying for the damage done to the natural environment.
  • Tradable Permits (Cap and Trade): Supports this principle by capping total emissions and forcing heavy polluters to buy permits from cleaner companies.
  • Challenges in Developing Economies: Strict enforcement of polluter-pays can cripple growing industries in developing nations that cannot afford clean technology, leading to job losses and economic instability.
HL.b.5

Greenwashing

Key Understanding“Greenwashing” or “green sheen” is where companies use marketing to give themselves a more environmentally friendly image.

Companies may spend more resources on advertising themselves as green than on actual environmentally sound practices. This misleads consumers who want to make sustainable choices.

Common Tactics:
  • Vague Language: Using unregulated terms like "eco-friendly," "natural," or "green" without certifications.
  • Hidden Trade-offs: Advertising recycled packaging while the product itself is highly toxic.
  • Irrelevant Claims: Boasting that a product is "CFC-Free" (CFCs have been globally banned for decades).

Combatting it: Regulatory bodies (like the FTC) can strictly define terms, mandate independent third-party certifications, and issue heavy fines for misleading advertising.

HL.b.6

The Tragedy of the Commons

Key UnderstandingThe tragedy of the commons highlights the problem where property rights are not clearly delineated and no market price is attached to a common good, resulting in overexploitation.

When a resource (like the atmosphere, open ocean fisheries, or a shared pasture) is open to everyone, individuals acting in their own self-interest will extract as much as possible, ultimately destroying the resource for everyone.

Alternative Solutions:

Traditional economics suggests either strict government regulation or full privatization. However, Elinor Ostrom won a Nobel Prize for demonstrating community-based resource management. Local communities often successfully manage shared resources sustainably by setting their own social norms, rules, and local sanctions without needing a central government.

HL.b.7 / HL.b.8

Environmental Accounting & Non-Use Value

HL.b.7 Environmental accounting is the attempt to attach economic value to natural resources and their depletion.

It integrates environmental costs (like pollution or loss of biodiversity) into a nation's financial reporting. The challenge is consensus value—different stakeholders disagree wildly on what a forest or wetland is "worth" in dollars.

HL.b.8 In some cases, economic value can be established by use, but this is not the case for non-use values.
Value TypeDefinition & Example
Use ValueDirectly using the resource (e.g. timber, fishing, hiking tourism). Easy to calculate market price.
Non-Use ValueValue attached to a resource just for existing, even if humans never visit it or use it (e.g. existence value of blue whales or the Amazon). Incredibly difficult to quantify economically.
HL.b.9 / HL.b.10

Ecological vs Environmental Economics

HL.b.9 Ecological economics is different from environmental economics in that it views the economy as a subsystem of Earth’s larger biosphere and the social system as being a sub- component of ecology.

While Environmental Economics tries to fix market failures using taxes within the current capitalist system, Ecological Economics states that infinite growth on a finite planet is impossible. If the biosphere collapses, the economy collapses.

HL.b.10 While the economic valuation of ecosystem services is addressed by environmental economics, there is an even greater emphasis in ecological economics.
  • Ecological economics attributes massive value to forests beyond timber: carbon sequestration, flood prevention, cultural heritage, and intrinsic value.
  • Resource Transfer Agreements: Policies where wealthy nations pay developing nations to protect their forests (e.g. REDD+). Challenges include corruption, monitoring, and infringing on indigenous land rights.
HL.b.11 / HL.b.12

Measuring Economic Growth

HL.b.11 Economic growth is the change in the total market value of goods and services in a country over a period and is usually measured as the annual percentage change in GDP.

Limitations of GDP (Gross Domestic Product): GDP only measures economic transactions. If a country cuts down all its forests and sells the wood, GDP goes up. If an oil spill happens and billions are spent cleaning it up, GDP goes up. It completely ignores environmental destruction and social inequality.

Alternative Metric — GPI: The Genuine Progress Indicator (GPI) subtracts the costs of negative effects (pollution, crime, resource depletion) from the GDP to provide a much more accurate assessment of a country's actual well-being.
HL.b.12 Economic growth is influenced by supply and demand, and may be perceived as a measure of prosperity.
HL.b.13 / HL.b.14

Eco-Economic Decoupling

HL.b.13 Economic growth has impacts on environmental welfare.

Historically, unchecked economic growth is directly tied to increased resource extraction, habitat destruction, and massive increases in greenhouse gas emissions.

HL.b.14 Eco-economic decoupling is the notion of separating economic growth from environmental degradation.

Relative Decoupling

The economy grows faster than the rate of environmental degradation. We are getting more efficient per unit of GDP, but total environmental impact is still going up.

Absolute Decoupling

The economy grows while total environmental degradation actually decreases. This is the holy grail of sustainability, but exceedingly difficult to achieve on a global scale.

HL.b.15 / HL.b.16

Degrowth and Zero Growth Models

HL.b.15 Ecological economics supports the need for degrowth, zero growth or slow growth, and advocates planned reduction in consumption and production, particularly in high-income countries.

Degrowth is a planned, deliberate reduction in the size of the economy to bring it back in balance with planetary boundaries.
Note: This is very different from a recession, which is an unplanned, chaotic economic collapse causing suffering.

  • Barriers to Degrowth: Intense political resistance, corporate lobbying, and the fact that modern capitalist systems are structurally reliant on perpetual growth to function.
HL.b.16 Ecological economists support a slow/no/zero growth model.

They argue that once a country reaches a certain level of wealth, more GDP does not increase happiness. We should transition to a "steady-state economy" that focuses on human well-being rather than consuming more stuff.

HL.b.17

Circular & Doughnut Economics

Key UnderstandingThe circular economy and doughnut economics models can be seen as applications of ecological economics for sustainability.
ModelExplanation
Circular EconomyReplaces the linear "Take-Make-Dispose" model. Aims to eliminate waste entirely by designing products to be endlessly repaired, reused, or recycled back into the system.
Doughnut EconomicsCreated by Kate Raworth. The goal is to live in the "doughnut" — the safe and just space for humanity. We must ensure everyone has the Social Foundation (food, water, equity) without overshooting the Ecological Ceiling (climate change, biodiversity loss).
