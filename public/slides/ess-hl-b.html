HL.b.1 / HL.b.2

Economics & The Environment

HL.b.1 Economics studies how humans produce, distribute and consume goods and services, both individually and collectively.

Supply and Demand: The fundamental principle of economics. Supply is how much of a resource is available, and Demand is how much people want it. Market Equilibrium is the point where the supply curve and demand curve intersect (the price people are willing to pay matches the price producers are willing to accept).

HL.b.2 Environmental economics is economics applied to the environment and environmental issues.

Environmental economists use tools like taxes, subsidies, and valuation to address environmental problems, often using market-based instruments like carbon taxes to change consumer and business behavior.

HL.b.3 / HL.b.4

Market Failure & Polluter Pays

HL.b.3 Market failure occurs when the allocation of goods and services by the free market imposes negative impacts on the environment.

Externalities: A cost (or benefit) caused by a producer that is not financially incurred by that producer. Negative externalities (like a factory polluting a river) cause market failure because the price of the factory's goods does not reflect the true cost to society (the polluted river).

HL.b.4 When the market fails to prevent negative impacts, the polluter-pays principle may be applied.

This principle states that those who produce pollution should bear the costs of managing it to prevent damage to human health or the environment. It is often implemented via Carbon Taxes, Tradable Permits (Cap-and-Trade), or fines.

HL.b.5

Greenwashing

Key Understanding"Greenwashing" or "green sheen" is where companies use marketing to give themselves a more environmentally friendly image.

Companies may spend more time and money marketing themselves as "eco-friendly" than actually minimizing their environmental impact. This misleads consumers who are trying to make sustainable choices.

Common Tactics:
  • Using vague terms like "all-natural" or "eco" without certification.
  • Highlighting one tiny green initiative while ignoring massive pollution elsewhere (e.g. an oil company advertising their small solar farm).
  • Using green packaging and nature imagery to imply sustainability.
HL.b.6

Tragedy of the Commons

Key UnderstandingThe tragedy of the commons highlights the problem where property rights are not clearly delineated and no market price is attached to a common good, resulting in overexploitation.

If a resource (like the open ocean, the atmosphere, or a shared pasture) is open to everyone, individuals acting in their own self-interest will over-use and destroy the resource, even though it is bad for everyone in the long run.

Alternative Solutions: While government regulation or privatization are common solutions, Elinor Ostrom (Nobel Laureate) proved that community-based resource management (local people creating their own rules and enforcing them) can be highly effective.
HL.b.7 / HL.b.8

Environmental Accounting

HL.b.7 Environmental accounting is the attempt to attach economic value to natural resources and their depletion.

By putting a dollar value on nature (e.g., valuing a forest for its timber, or a wetland for its flood protection), we can integrate the environment into a nation's financial reporting.

HL.b.8 In some cases, economic value can be established by use, but this is not the case for non-use values.
  • Use Value: The value of using a resource directly (e.g. chopping down a tree for wood).
  • Non-Use Value: The value attached to a resource just for existing, even if humans never use it (e.g. the existence value of the Amazon rainforest or blue whales). These are incredibly difficult to quantify in a spreadsheet.
HL.b.9 / HL.b.10

Ecological Economics vs Environmental Economics

HL.b.9 Ecological economics is different from environmental economics in that it views the economy as a subsystem of Earth’s larger biosphere.

Environmental Economics

Views the environment as a subset of the economy (a resource to be used and managed). Focuses on fixing market failures (taxes, subsidies) to achieve sustainability within the current capitalist system.

Ecological Economics

Views the economy as a subsystem entirely dependent on the Earth's biosphere. If the biosphere collapses, the economy collapses. Emphasizes absolute limits to growth and the true valuation of ecosystem services (HL.b.10).

HL.b.11 / HL.b.12

Measuring Growth

HL.b.11 Economic growth is the change in the total market value of goods and services in a country over a period and is usually measured as the annual percentage change in GDP.

GDP (Gross Domestic Product) has severe limitations: it measures economic activity, but not well-being or environmental health. Cutting down an entire forest and selling the wood increases GDP, but destroys natural capital.

Alternative metrics like the Genuine Progress Indicator (GPI) subtract the costs of environmental degradation and crime from the GDP to give a more accurate picture of prosperity.

HL.b.12 Economic growth is influenced by supply and demand, and may be perceived as a measure of prosperity.
HL.b.13 / HL.b.14

Decoupling Growth from Degradation

HL.b.13 Economic growth has impacts on environmental welfare.

Historically, unchecked economic growth is directly tied to increased greenhouse gas emissions, resource depletion, and habitat destruction.

HL.b.14 Eco-economic decoupling is the notion of separating economic growth from environmental degradation.
  • Relative Decoupling: The economy grows faster than the rate of environmental degradation (we are getting more efficient, but total impact is still rising).
  • Absolute Decoupling: The economy grows while total environmental degradation actually decreases. This is the ultimate goal, but is extremely difficult to achieve.
HL.b.15 / HL.b.16

Degrowth & Zero Growth

HL.b.15 Ecological economics supports the need for degrowth, zero growth or slow growth, and advocates planned reduction in consumption and production, particularly in high-income countries.

Because the Earth has finite resources, infinite economic growth is mathematically impossible. Degrowth is a planned, deliberate reduction in the size of the economy to bring it back in balance with planetary boundaries (unlike a recession, which is an unplanned economic collapse).

HL.b.16 Ecological economists support a slow/no/zero growth model.

Conventional economists believe technology will allow us to grow forever (technocentrism). Ecological economists argue we must transition to a steady-state economy (zero growth) focused on human well-being rather than consuming more stuff.

HL.b.17

Circular & Doughnut Economics

Key UnderstandingThe circular economy and doughnut economics models can be seen as applications of ecological economics for sustainability.

Circular Economy

Moving away from the linear "Take-Make-Dispose" model. A circular economy aims to eliminate waste entirely by ensuring all products are designed to be reused, repaired, or endlessly recycled back into the system.

Doughnut Economics

A model by Kate Raworth. The goal is to live in the "doughnut" — the safe and just space for humanity. We must ensure everyone has the Social Foundation (food, water, equity) without overshooting the Ecological Ceiling (climate change, biodiversity loss).
