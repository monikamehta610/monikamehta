Primary Defenses: The First Line

Key UnderstandingPathogens cause infectious diseases. The skin and mucous membranes form primary barriers against these pathogens. Blood clotting seals cuts to prevent entry.

Our bodies are constantly exposed to pathogens—microorganisms that cause disease (like bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protists). Our first line of defense is purely physical and chemical.

Skin

A tough, continuous physical barrier.

  • Contains sebaceous glands that secrete sebum (lowers pH to inhibit bacterial growth).
  • Tough outer layers of dead cells.

Mucous Membranes

Sticky traps in vulnerable areas.

  • Found in airways, nose, and digestive tract.
  • Secretes mucus which physically traps pathogens.
  • Contains lysozyme (an antibacterial enzyme).

C3.2.4 - C3.2.5

Innate vs. Adaptive Immunity

Key UnderstandingThe innate immune system is non-specific and constant. The adaptive immune system is specific and builds memory.

If a pathogen breaches the primary barriers, the internal immune system takes over. It is split into two broad categories:

PropertyInnate Immune SystemAdaptive Immune System
Specificity Non-specific (treats all pathogens roughly the same) Highly specific (targets individual pathogen strains)
Memory No memory (response is exactly the same every time) Builds memory (response gets faster/stronger upon reinfection)
Key Players Phagocytes (Macrophages, Neutrophils) Lymphocytes (B-cells, T-cells)

Phagocytosis (Innate Response)

White blood cells called phagocytes engulf pathogens via endocytosis. Once inside the phagocyte, the pathogen is trapped in a vesicle which fuses with a lysosome. Digestive enzymes completely destroy the pathogen.

The Adaptive Response: Clonal Selection

Key UnderstandingLymphocytes mount a specific adaptive immune response. B-cells multiply by clonal selection to form antibody-secreting plasma cells and memory cells.

When the innate system cannot handle an infection, the adaptive system steps in. This process relies on antigens—foreign molecules (usually proteins on the surface of a pathogen) that trigger an immune response.

  • Plasma Cells: Short-lived cell factories that pump out massive amounts of specific antibodies.
  • Memory Cells: Long-lived cells that remain in the blood. If the same pathogen attacks again, they trigger a rapid and overwhelming secondary response (this is immunity).
C3.2.13 - C3.2.14

Antibiotics & Resistance

Key UnderstandingAntibiotics block processes that occur in prokaryotic cells but not in eukaryotic cells. Evolution of resistance is a major problem.

Antibiotics are chemicals that kill or inhibit the growth of bacteria (prokaryotes). They work by targeting metabolic processes unique to bacteria, such as:

  • Cell wall synthesis (e.g., Penicillin targeting peptidoglycan).
  • Protein synthesis via 70S ribosomes.
  • DNA replication enzymes specific to bacteria.
Application: Viral Infections

Viruses are non-living and rely entirely on the host's (eukaryotic) cellular machinery to reproduce. Because antibiotics only target prokaryotic features, antibiotics cannot be used to treat viral infections!

Skill: Natural Selection & Resistance

Due to genetic variation (from mutation or plasmid transfer), some bacteria may possess natural resistance to an antibiotic. When an antibiotic is used, it acts as a selective pressure. Susceptible bacteria die, while resistant bacteria survive, reproduce, and pass on the resistance trait. Over time, the entire strain becomes resistant (e.g., MRSA).

C3.2.11 - C3.2.12

HIV and the Immune System

Key UnderstandingTransmission of HIV occurs through body fluids. HIV destroys helper T-lymphocytes, leading to AIDS.

The Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) is a retrovirus that directly attacks the immune system, leading to a catastrophic breakdown of the body's defense.

Mechanism of Attack

HIV specifically targets and infects Helper T-cells. Over time, as the virus replicates, it destroys these cells. Without Helper T-cells, B-cells cannot be activated to produce antibodies.

AIDS

When the number of active Helper T-cells drops below a critical threshold, the patient develops Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS). The patient loses the ability to fight off even mild opportunistic infections.

Transmission: HIV is transmitted through the exchange of bodily fluids, primarily unprotected sexual intercourse, sharing of hypodermic needles, blood transfusions, or from mother to child across the placenta/breast milk.
C3.2.16 - C3.2.18

Vaccines and Public Health

Key UnderstandingVaccines induce immunity without causing the disease. Herd immunity protects the vulnerable.

Vaccines contain antigens (attenuated pathogens, dead pathogens, or purified surface proteins) that trigger an immune response without causing the actual disease. This leads to the production of memory cells, granting immunity.

Application: Herd Immunity

When a sufficiently high percentage of a population is immunized (typically $>80\%$ or $>90\%$, depending on the disease's $R_0$), the chain of transmission is broken. This provides indirect protection to individuals who cannot be vaccinated (e.g., newborns, immunocompromised individuals).

Skill: Evaluating COVID-19 Data

Students should be able to analyze epidemiological data, graphs, and statistics regarding the spread of SARS-CoV-2. Look for trends like $R_t$ (effective reproduction number), the impact of vaccination rollouts on hospitalization rates, and the emergence of variants over time.

Zoonoses (C3.2.15)

Many major human diseases originated as zoonoses—pathogens that cross the species barrier from animals to humans. Examples include COVID-19 (bats/pangolins), HIV (chimpanzees), and Ebola (fruit bats).
