Differentiation and Gene Expression

Key Understanding Specialized tissues can develop by cell differentiation in multicellular organisms, which involves the expression of some genes and not others in a cell's genome.

All diploid cells in a multicellular organism share an identical genome. However, they develop into distinct, specialized cell types (like neurons, muscle cells, and skin cells) through a process called differentiation.

Fertilized egg (Zygote) divides via mitosis
↓
Cells begin to receive different chemical signals based on their position
↓

↓
Cells become specialized in their form and function (Differentiation)

Morphogens and chemical gradients play a crucial role in regulating which genes are expressed during early embryonic development, ensuring tissues form in the correct locations.

Stem Cells

Key Understanding Stem cells are unspecialized cells that can divide continuously and have the capacity to differentiate into specialized cell types.

Stem cells retain the capacity to divide and have the unique ability to differentiate along different pathways.

Totipotent

Can differentiate into any cell type, including extra-embryonic tissues (placenta).

  • Example: The early zygote.

Pluripotent

Can differentiate into any cell type in the body, but not extra-embryonic tissues.

  • Example: Inner cell mass of a blastocyst.

Multipotent

Can differentiate into a limited number of related cell types.

  • Example: Hematopoietic (blood) stem cells in bone marrow.
Application Stem cell niches (like bone marrow or hair follicles) maintain stem cell populations, allowing for continuous tissue repair and regeneration throughout an organism's life.

Surface Area-to-Volume Ratio Constraints

Key Understanding The surface area-to-volume ratio (SA:V) acts as a constraint on cell size.

As a cell grows larger, its volume increases faster than its surface area. This mathematical relationship places a strict limit on how large a cell can become while remaining functional.

Mathematical Relationship:

For a spherical cell of radius $r$:

  • Surface Area ($SA$) = $4\pi r^2$
  • Volume ($V$) = $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$

The SA:V ratio simplifies to: $\frac{SA}{V} = \frac{3}{r}$. Thus, as $r$ increases, the ratio decreases.

Skill: Models in Science Using cubes of different sizes to model cells is a practical way to calculate and visualize how increasing size dramatically lowers the SA:V ratio. A 1cm cube has a SA:V of 6:1, whereas a 3cm cube has a SA:V of only 2:1.

A low SA:V ratio means the cell's membrane (surface area) cannot efficiently exchange enough resources (like O$_2$ and glucose) or excrete enough waste (like CO$_2$) to support the metabolic demands of its large internal volume.

Structural Adaptations of Cells

Key Understanding Specialized cells have structural adaptations that facilitate their specific functions.
Cell TypeKey Structural AdaptationsFunction
Type I Pneumocytes Extremely thin, flattened shape. Increases SA:V ratio and minimizes diffusion distance. Rapid gas exchange (O$_2$ and CO$_2$) in the alveoli.
Type II Pneumocytes Cuboidal shape with secretory vesicles (lamellar bodies). Secrete pulmonary surfactant to reduce alveolar surface tension.
Sperm Cell (Male Gamete) Highly streamlined, possesses a flagellum, packed with mitochondria, and contains an acrosome. Motility to reach the egg; penetrating the egg's zona pellucida.
Egg Cell (Female Gamete) Very large volume, containing abundant cytoplasm, nutrient stores (yolk), and cortical granules. Supports early embryonic development after fertilization.
Striated Muscle Fiber Long, cylindrical, multinucleated, and packed with contractile myofibrils (actin and myosin). Powerful, coordinated voluntary contractions.
