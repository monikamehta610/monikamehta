Gas Exchange and Surface Area

Key Understanding Gas exchange is vital for living organisms. Evolving larger bodies poses a challenge due to decreasing surface area-to-volume ratio ($SA:V$).

For efficient diffusion, respiratory surfaces must maximize their $SA:V$ ratio. We can express the rate of diffusion using Fick's Law of Diffusion:

$Rate \propto \frac{Surface Area \times \Delta Concentration}{Distance}$
  • Permeability: Surfaces must allow O₂ and CO₂ to pass through easily.
  • Thin tissue layers: Reduces the diffusion distance.
  • Moisture: Gases must dissolve in liquid to diffuse across cell membranes.
  • Large Surface Area: Essential for absorbing and releasing sufficient volumes of gas to support metabolism.
B3.1.3 / B3.1.4

Mammalian Lungs & Concentration Gradients

Key Understanding Mammalian lungs are adapted for gas exchange via alveoli, and maintain steep concentration gradients.

Ventilation (breathing) and continuous blood flow ensure that concentration gradients of O₂ and CO₂ are maintained.

Adaptations of Lungs

  • Branched bronchioles increase surface area.
  • Extensive capillary beds wrap around alveoli.
  • Surfactant prevents alveolar collapse by reducing surface tension.

Maintaining Gradients

  • Ventilation continuously replaces stale air with fresh air.
  • Blood flow quickly removes oxygenated blood, replacing it with deoxygenated blood.
B3.1.5

Ventilation of the Lungs

Key Understanding Ventilation relies on muscle contractions creating pressure changes.

Breathing follows Boyle's Law: $P \propto \frac{1}{V}$. Changing the volume of the thoracic cavity inversely changes the pressure, drawing air in or pushing it out.

Inhalation: Diaphragm contracts (flattens), external intercostal muscles pull ribs up and out.
↓
Thoracic cavity volume increases $\rightarrow$ pressure decreases below atmospheric pressure.
↓
Air flows INTO the lungs down the pressure gradient.
Application: Antagonistic Muscle Action

Inhalation and exhalation require antagonistic muscle pairs. For example, the external and internal intercostal muscles work opposite each other.

B3.1.6

Measurement of Lung Volumes

Key Understanding Lung volumes can be measured to analyze physiological parameters.

Spirometry is used to measure the amount of air inhaled and exhaled.

  • Tidal Volume ($V_T$): Volume of air breathed in and out during a normal resting breath.
  • Vital Capacity: The maximum volume of air that can be exhaled after a maximum inhalation.
  • Inspiratory/Expiratory Reserves: Extra volume that can be inhaled/exhaled beyond the tidal volume.
Skill: Data Analysis

Be prepared to calculate ventilation rate using the formula: $Ventilation \ Rate = Tidal \ Volume \times Breathing \ Rate$.

B3.1.7 / B3.1.8

Leaf Adaptations for Gas Exchange

Key Understanding Leaves are adapted for photosynthesis and gas exchange.

Gas exchange in plants involves CO₂ entering for photosynthesis and O₂ exiting as a byproduct. Water vapor also exits via transpiration.

  • Waxy Cuticle: Prevents excessive water loss across the epidermis.
  • Spongy Mesophyll: Contains air spaces for rapid diffusion of gases within the leaf.
  • Stomatal Guard Cells: Open and close stomatal pores to regulate gas exchange and limit water loss.
Skill: Plan Diagrams

You must be able to draw and label a plan diagram of a transverse section of a dicotyledonous leaf, showing tissue layers (epidermis, palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll, and veins/vascular bundles).

B3.1.9 / B3.1.10

Transpiration and Stomatal Density

Key Understanding Transpiration is an inevitable consequence of gas exchange.

When stomata open to allow CO₂ in, water vapor inevitably escapes. This creates the transpirational pull that draws water up the xylem.

Stomata open for carbon dioxide diffusion.
↓
Water vapor diffuses out of the moist air spaces (Transpiration).
↓
Water is drawn from xylem into mesophyll cells to replace lost water.
Skill: Stomatal Density

You should be able to measure stomatal density from leaf peels or micrographs. Higher density typically correlates with higher rates of gas exchange (and potential water loss).

B3.1.11 / B3.1.12 HL Only

Haemoglobin and the Bohr Shift

Key Understanding (HL) Structural differences in haemoglobin match functional requirements for oxygen transport.

Haemoglobin (Hb) binds to oxygen forming oxyhaemoglobin. The affinity for oxygen changes based on the environment.

Foetal vs Adult Haemoglobin

Foetal Hb has a higher affinity for O₂ than adult Hb. This allows the foetus to pull O₂ away from the mother's blood across the placenta.

The Bohr Shift

High CO₂ lowers blood pH. This causes an allosteric change in Hb, decreasing its affinity for O₂, leading to more O₂ unloading in respiring tissues.

B3.1.13 HL Only

Oxygen Dissociation Curves

Key Understanding (HL) Oxygen dissociation curves represent haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen.

The curve is sigmoidal (S-shaped) due to cooperative binding—as one O₂ molecule binds, the affinity for subsequent O₂ molecules increases.

  • Right Shift (Bohr Effect): Caused by high CO₂/low pH. Hb releases more O₂ to tissues.
  • Left Shift: Caused by low CO₂ or in foetal Hb. Hb holds onto O₂ more tightly.
Application: High Altitude

At high altitudes, lower $pO_2$ leads to reduced oxygen saturation. Acclimatization involves increased red blood cell production and a right shift in the dissociation curve to enhance O₂ delivery to tissues.
