D1.2 Protein Synthesis

IB Biology 2025 Syllabus

Understanding how the genetic code is translated into functional proteins.

Central Dogma of Molecular Biology

The flow of genetic information within a biological system is generally DNA → RNA → Protein.

  • Transcription: DNA is used as a template to synthesize messenger RNA (mRNA).
  • Translation: mRNA is used as a template to synthesize a polypeptide chain (protein).

Transcription (SL & HL)

Transcription is the synthesis of mRNA copied from the DNA base sequences by RNA polymerase.

The process occurs in a $5' \rightarrow 3'$ direction. RNA polymerase binds to a site on the DNA at the start of a gene, moves along the gene separating DNA into single strands, and pairs up RNA nucleotides with complementary bases on one strand of the DNA. There is no thymine in RNA, so uracil pairs with adenine. RNA polymerase forms covalent bonds between the RNA nucleotides.

Sense vs Antisense Strand

The antisense strand is the template. The sense strand has the same sequence as the mRNA (with T instead of U).

Skill: Deducing Sequences

Given the antisense strand 3'-TAC-CGA-TTC-5', the mRNA sequence is 5'-AUG-GCU-AAG-3'.

Post-Transcriptional Modification (HL Only)

In eukaryotes, the immediate product of transcription is pre-mRNA. It must be modified to form mature mRNA.

  • Capping: Addition of a 5' methyl-guanosine cap.
  • Polyadenylation: Addition of a 3' poly-A tail.
  • Splicing: Removal of introns (non-coding regions) and joining of exons (coding regions).

Alternative splicing can produce different mature mRNAs from a single gene, increasing the proteome complexity.

Translation (SL & HL)

Translation is the synthesis of polypeptides on ribosomes.

The amino acid sequence of polypeptides is determined by mRNA according to the genetic code. Codons of three bases on mRNA correspond to one amino acid in a polypeptide.

tRNA & Anticodons

tRNA molecules have an anticodon that is complementary to an mRNA codon, and they carry the corresponding amino acid.

Translation depends on complementary base pairing between codons on mRNA and anticodons on tRNA.

Stages of Translation (HL Emphasis)

Translation consists of initiation, elongation, translocation, and termination.

  • Initiation: Assembly of the components that carry out the process (mRNA, tRNA, ribosome).
  • Elongation: Successive addition of amino acids to the growing polypeptide.
  • Translocation: Movement of the ribosome along the mRNA.
  • Termination: When a stop codon is reached, the complex disassembles.

Application: Antibiotics

Many antibiotics (e.g., tetracycline) target prokaryotic ribosomes (70S) specifically, inhibiting bacterial translation without affecting eukaryotic ribosomes (80S).

Post-Translational Modification (HL Only)

Polypeptides often require modification to become functional proteins.

These modifications can include:

  • Folding (assisted by chaperonins).
  • Cleavage (e.g., insulin precursor).
  • Addition of non-protein components (e.g., glycosylation to form glycoproteins).
  • Formation of quaternary structure (e.g., hemoglobin).

Check Your Understanding

Test your knowledge of D1.2 Protein Synthesis:

1. In what direction does transcription occur?

Transcription occurs in a $5' \rightarrow 3'$ direction.

2. What is the role of the promoter region in transcription? (HL)

The promoter is a non-coding sequence of DNA that serves as the binding site for RNA polymerase and determines which strand will act as the template.

3. Differentiate between introns and exons. (HL)

Introns are non-coding sequences that are removed during splicing, while exons are coding sequences that are joined together to form mature mRNA.

4. How many bases make up a single codon?

Three bases make up a single codon, which codes for one amino acid.

5. What is alternative splicing and why is it significant? (HL)

Alternative splicing is the process where some exons are removed along with introns. It is significant because it allows a single gene to code for multiple different proteins.
