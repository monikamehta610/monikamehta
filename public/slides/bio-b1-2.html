B1.2.1

Amino Acid Structure

Key Understanding Generalized structure of an amino acid.

Amino acids are the monomers (building blocks) of proteins. There are 20 different amino acids used by ribosomes to make polypeptides.

Generalized Formula

All amino acids share a common central core but differ in their R-group.

H₂N—CH(R)—COOH

Central Carbon ($\alpha$-carbon) bonded to:

  • Amine Group: (-NH₂)
  • Carboxyl Group: (-COOH)
  • Hydrogen Atom: (-H)
  • Variable R-group: Determines the specific properties of the amino acid.
Skill

Draw the generalized structure of an amino acid, clearly labeling the amine group, carboxyl group, and R-group.

B1.2.2

Condensation Reactions

Key Understanding Condensation reactions form dipeptides and longer polypeptide chains.

Amino acids are linked together on the ribosome to form polypeptides through condensation reactions. This involves the removal of a water molecule (H₂O).

Carboxyl group (-COOH) of amino acid 1
+
Amine group (-NH₂) of amino acid 2
↓
Peptide bond (-C-N-) forms + Water (H₂O) released

The resulting covalent bond between the amino acids is called a peptide bond. Multiple amino acids linked together form a polypeptide.

Application

Understanding that anabolic reactions, such as protein synthesis, require an input of energy (ATP) and release water.

Diet & Diversity

B1.2.3 Dietary requirements for amino acids.
B1.2.4 The infinite variety of possible peptide chains.

Essential Amino Acids

Out of the 20 common amino acids, 9 cannot be synthesized by the human body and must be obtained from the diet. These are essential amino acids. A lack of these leads to protein deficiency diseases.

Infinite Variety

Ribosomes can link the 20 amino acids together in any sequence. For a polypeptide of length $n$, there are $20^n$ possible sequences. This allows for virtually infinite diversity of protein structures and functions.

B1.2.5

Denaturation

Key Understanding The effect of pH and temperature on protein structure.

Denaturation is a structural change in a protein that results in the loss (usually permanent) of its biological properties. Because protein function depends heavily on its 3D shape, losing this shape destroys functionality.

  • Temperature: High thermal energy breaks the weak hydrogen bonds holding the secondary and tertiary structures together. The protein unfolds.
  • pH Extremes: Strong acids or alkalis alter the charge of R-groups, breaking ionic bonds within the protein structure and causing it to denature.
Exam Tip: Denaturation does not break peptide bonds (primary structure). It only disrupts the folding (secondary, tertiary, quaternary structures).

R-Groups & Primary Structure (HL)

B1.2.6 (HL) Chemical diversity in R-groups as the basis for diversity in protein form/function.
B1.2.7 (HL) Impact of primary structure on conformation.

The properties of a protein are ultimately determined by its primary structure (the exact sequence of amino acids) and their diverse R-groups.

R-Group TypeInteraction Behavior
Polar (Hydrophilic) Form hydrogen bonds; usually found on the exterior of soluble proteins.
Non-Polar (Hydrophobic) Cluster in the core of globular proteins, avoiding water.
Charged (Acidic/Basic) Form ionic bonds with oppositely charged R-groups.
Sulfur-containing Can form strong covalent disulfide bonds (bridges) for stability.

Because the primary structure defines the sequence of these specific R-groups, it entirely dictates how the protein will fold into its 3D conformation.

Secondary & Tertiary Structure (HL)

Secondary Structure (B1.2.8): Pleating and coiling. Hydrogen bonds form between the main peptide backbone (not R-groups) to create $\alpha$-helices or $\beta$-pleated sheets.
↓
Tertiary Structure (B1.2.9/10): 3D folding driven by R-group interactions.

Bonds in Tertiary Structure:

  • Hydrogen bonds (between polar R-groups)
  • Ionic bonds (between charged R-groups)
  • Disulfide covalent bonds (between cysteines)
  • Hydrophobic interactions (non-polar R-groups clustering in the center)

Quaternary Structure & Protein Types (HL)

B1.2.11 (HL) Quaternary structure of non-conjugated and conjugated proteins.

Some proteins consist of more than one polypeptide chain linked together. A conjugated protein contains a non-protein prosthetic group (e.g., the heme group in hemoglobin). Non-conjugated proteins consist only of polypeptides (e.g., collagen).

B1.2.12 (HL) Relationship of form and function in globular and fibrous proteins.

Globular Proteins

Folded into spherical shapes. Generally soluble in water.

  • Non-polar amino acids in core, polar on exterior.
  • Active roles: Enzymes, antibodies, hormones (e.g., Insulin, Hemoglobin).

Fibrous Proteins

Long, narrow strands. Generally insoluble in water.

  • Highly repetitive sequences.
  • Structural roles: Support, strength (e.g., Collagen, Keratin).
