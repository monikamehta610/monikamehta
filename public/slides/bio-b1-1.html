Carbon and Condensation Reactions

Key Understanding Carbon atoms can form four stable covalent bonds, allowing for the formation of diverse macromolecules. These are built via condensation reactions.

Carbon is the backbone of biological molecules. Because it has four valence electrons, it can form up to four covalent bonds with other elements (such as H, O, N, P), creating complex chains, rings, and branches.

Monomers: Single subunits (e.g., monosaccharides)
↓

Polymer + H₂O is released
Application Condensation reactions are anabolic (build up complex molecules). A water molecule (H₂O) is always a byproduct when two monomers join.
B1.1.3

Hydrolysis: Breaking Down Polymers

Key Understanding Digestion of polymers into monomers occurs via hydrolysis reactions.

When organisms consume food, the large macromolecules (polymers) must be broken down into smaller monomers to be absorbed and used by cells. This process is called hydrolysis (hydro = water, lysis = to break).

  • Requires the addition of water (H₂O) to break the covalent bonds.
  • Catalyzed by specific digestive enzymes (e.g., amylase for starch, lipase for lipids).
  • This is a catabolic reaction (breaking down).
Skill: Drawing Reactions Be able to identify and draw simplified models of condensation and hydrolysis reactions, clearly showing where H and OH are removed or added to form H₂O.
B1.1.4 / B1.1.5

Carbohydrates: Energy and Structure

Monosaccharides (B1.1.4)

Single sugar units, primarily used as an immediate energy source for cellular respiration.

  • Examples: Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), Fructose, Galactose, Ribose.
  • Highly soluble in water, easily transported in blood.

Polysaccharides (B1.1.5)

Long chains of monosaccharides, used for energy storage.

  • Starch: Energy storage in plants (amylose & amylopectin).
  • Glycogen: Energy storage in animals (highly branched).

By storing glucose as large, insoluble polysaccharides, cells prevent osmosis issues (water entering the cell and causing it to burst) while keeping energy reserves dense.

B1.1.6 / B1.1.7

Structural Carbohydrates & Glycoproteins

Cellulose in Plants (B1.1.6)

Cellulose is a polysaccharide made of $\beta$-glucose. Because the glucose units alternate their orientation, cellulose forms straight, unbranched chains.

  • These chains hydrogen bond together to form rigid microfibrils.
  • Provides immense tensile strength to plant cell walls, preventing them from bursting under high water pressure (turgor).

Glycoproteins in Cell Recognition (B1.1.7)

Proteins embedded in cell membranes often have short carbohydrate chains attached (glycoproteins). These are critical for:

  • Cell-Cell Recognition: Allowing the immune system to identify "self" vs. "non-self" cells.
  • Receptors: Acting as binding sites for hormones or other signalling molecules.

Lipids: Hydrophobic Molecules

Key Understanding Lipids are a diverse group of carbon compounds characterized by their hydrophobic properties. Triglycerides and phospholipids form via condensation.

Unlike carbohydrates, lipids do not dissolve in water (they are hydrophobic/non-polar). They include fats, oils, waxes, and steroids.

Types of Fatty Acids

TypeStructureProperties at Room Temp
Saturated No double bonds between carbon atoms (max hydrogen). Straight chains. Solid (e.g., butter, animal fat). Pack tightly together.
Monounsaturated One double bond (C=C). Causes a "kink" in the chain. Liquid (e.g., olive oil). Do not pack tightly.
Polyunsaturated Two or more double bonds (C=C). Multiple kinks. Liquid (e.g., sunflower oil, fish oil).
Skill: Molecular Identification You must be able to visually identify saturated vs. unsaturated fatty acids from molecular diagrams based on the presence of double carbon bonds.
B1.1.11

Energy Storage: Lipids vs Carbohydrates

Key Understanding Triglycerides in adipose tissue act as long-term energy storage and insulation.

Triglycerides are stored in specialized cells called adipose tissue in animals. Why use lipids instead of carbohydrates for long-term storage?

  • Energy Density: Lipids contain roughly twice the amount of energy per gram ($\sim 38 \text{ kJ/g}$) compared to carbohydrates ($\sim 17 \text{ kJ/g}$).
  • Weight: Lipids are stored pure, without associated water. Carbohydrates are stored with large amounts of water, making them heavy. This is crucial for mobile animals!
  • Insulation: Adipose tissue acts as a thermal insulator against heat loss (e.g., blubber in whales).
B1.1.12 / B1.1.13

Phospholipids and Steroids

The Phospholipid Bilayer (B1.1.12)

Phospholipids are amphipathic—they have a hydrophilic (water-loving) phosphate head and two hydrophobic (water-fearing) fatty acid tails. In water, they spontaneously arrange into a bilayer:

  • Heads face outward towards the aqueous environment.
  • Tails face inward, shielded from the water.
  • This creates the fundamental structure of all cell membranes!

Steroid Hormones (B1.1.13)

Application Because steroid hormones (like testosterone and estrogen) are lipids and inherently hydrophobic, they can pass directly through the hydrophobic core of the phospholipid bilayer to enter target cells.
