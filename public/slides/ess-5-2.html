5.2.1 – 5.2.3

Land Scarcity, Equity & Food Distribution

Key UnderstandingArable land is a finite resource. Global agriculture produces enough food to feed 8+ billion people, but distribution is inequitable and ~30% is wasted.

🗑️ Global Food Waste (~30%)

Developing nations lose food post-harvest due to storage & transport gaps; MEDCs waste food at retail and consumer levels.

⚖️ Inequitable Access

Marginalized groups (such as indigenous pastoralists) are disproportionately displaced by commercial land-use decisions without input.

Case Study: Maasai Pastoralist Dispossession (East Africa)

Traditional Maasai grazing lands have been increasingly converted to commercial agriculture, national parks, and safari tourism, restricting their seasonal nomadic mobility and creating localized food insecurity.

5.2.4 – 5.2.6

Agricultural Systems & Traditional Practices

Key UnderstandingFarming systems vary widely based on soil, climate, and culture. Traditional practices like nomadic pastoralism and slash-and-burn sustain low-density populations.
Farming SystemInput & Energy IntensityOutputs & Sustainability
Subsistence FarmingLow inputs (family labor, manure, saved seed)Local family food supply; high ecological sustainability at low density
Nomadic PastoralismLow inputs (seasonal livestock herding)Meat, milk, blood; sustainable in arid grasslands with low population
Slash-and-Burn (Shifting)Low inputs (forest clearance & burning)Ash-fertilized crops for 2-3 yrs; forest regenerates if fallow period is long
Commercial MonocultureHigh inputs (fossil fuels, synthetic NPK, pesticides, hybrid seeds)High single-crop yield for market; high risk of soil degradation & pollution
5.2.7

The Green Revolution

Key UnderstandingThe Green Revolution doubled food yields through high-yielding crop varieties (HYVs), synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, and expansion of irrigation.

✅ Benefits

  • Saved millions from acute famine in India, Mexico, and Southeast Asia
  • Dramatically increased crop yield per hectare without expanding land area
  • Lowered global grain market prices

⚠️ Environmental & Social Costs

  • Heavy soil degradation, salinization, and chemical runoff eutrophication
  • High fossil fuel dependency & groundwater depletion
  • Smallholder farmer debt traps due to expensive patented seeds & inputs
5.2.8 & 5.2.9

Synthetic Fertilizers & 6 Soil Conservation Techniques

Key UnderstandingSynthetic fertilizers boost yields at the expense of soil health. Sustainable agriculture utilizes specific soil conservation techniques to preserve topsoil.
TechniqueMechanism & How It WorksKey Environmental Benefit
TerracingCarving step-like flat benches into steep hillsidesPrevents severe water runoff and soil washing downhill
Contour PlowingPlowing furrows horizontally across land contoursSlows surface runoff water speed, trapping sediment
Cover CroppingPlanting clover or rye between main harvest seasonsProtects bare topsoil from wind/water erosion; adds organic matter
Crop RotationAlternating grain crops with nitrogen-fixing legumesBreaks pest cycles and naturally replenishes soil nitrates
Shelterbelts / WindbreaksPlanting rows of tall trees along field bordersDeflects wind, preventing topsoil wind erosion
Zero Tillage (No-Till)Seeding directly into unplowed crop residuePreserves soil structure, earthworms, and soil carbon storage
5.2.10

Trophic Efficiency & Diet Sustainability

Key UnderstandingDiets lower in trophic levels (plant-based) are significantly more energy-efficient and sustainable due to ~90% energy loss between trophic levels.

Virtual Water & Land Footprint per kg Food

  • 1 kg Beef: ~15,400 Liters of water + 10-20× more land than grains (Trophic Level 3/4)
  • 1 kg Pork / Chicken: ~4,300 - 6,000 Liters of water
  • 1 kg Wheat / Cereals: ~1,500 Liters of water (Trophic Level 1 - Producer)
  • 1 kg Vegetables / Potatoes: ~290 - 400 Liters of water
Energy Loss Rule: Eating grain directly feeds 10× more people than feeding the grain to cattle and consuming the beef.
5.2.11 & 5.2.12

Sustainable Supply & The 4 Pillars of Food Security

Key UnderstandingFood security exists when all people have physical and economic access to safe, nutritious food. Requires sustainable intensification without expanding farmland.
Pillar of Food SecurityFocus & Definition
1. AvailabilitySufficient global/local food production supply
2. AccessEconomic affordability and physical access to market supplies
3. UtilizationNutritional quality, clean water, and sanitation for proper absorption
4. StabilityConsistent food access over time without climate or conflict disruptions
HL — 5.2.14 & 5.2.15

Regenerative Agriculture & PermacultureHL

Key UnderstandingAlternative systems like permaculture, regenerative farming, and mob grazing restore soil health, sequester carbon, and boost biodiversity.

🌿 Permaculture Principles

Design agricultural systems modeled on natural ecosystems: polycultures, closed nutrient loops, rainwater harvesting, zero chemical inputs.

🐂 Regenerative Mob Grazing

Concentrating livestock in small paddocks for short periods mimics wild herds, trampling organic matter into soil and building topsoil carbon.

HL — 5.2.16

High-Tech & Vertical AgricultureHL

Key UnderstandingModern indoor high-tech greenhouses, hydroponics, and urban vertical farms achieve extreme productivity per m² with minimal water use.
TechnologyMechanism & AdvantagesKey Trade-offs

HL — 5.2.17 – 5.2.20

Diet Sustainability, Wild Harvest & MalnutritionHL

Key UnderstandingDietary footprint varies widely. Malnutrition encompasses both undernourishment (hunger) and overnourishment (obesity).

⚠️ Undernourishment (~735M)

Chronic caloric deficit causing stunting & wasting in impoverished, climate-shocked, or war-torn regions.

🍔 Overnourishment (~1.9B)

Excess caloric intake from cheap, ultra-processed, energy-dense foods leading to obesity and cardiovascular diseases.

EAT-Lancet Planetary Health Diet: Recommends 50% reduction in global red meat and sugar consumption, doubling fruit, vegetable, legume, and nut intake.

Exam Preparation

Key Takeaways & Exam Tips

Must-Know Concepts

  • Food Equity: Global agriculture produces enough food; starvation stems from poor distribution, poverty, and 30% food waste.
  • Green Revolution Trade-off: High crop yields vs soil salinization, chemical runoff, and farmer debt.
  • 6 Soil Conservation Methods: Terracing, Contour Plowing, Cover Cropping, Crop Rotation, Shelterbelts, Zero Tillage.
  • Trophic Energy: Eating plant-based foods directly saves 90% energy compared to livestock consumption.
  • HL Malnutrition Spectrum: Contrast undernourishment (hunger) with overnourishment (obesity).
Exam tip: When comparing commercial monoculture to permaculture in 9-mark essay questions, evaluate inputs (fossil fuels vs labor), outputs (single commodity vs diverse food), and long-term soil sustainability.
