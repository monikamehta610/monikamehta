C1.2.1

ATP as the Energy Currency

Key UnderstandingATP is the molecule used by cells to supply immediate energy for cellular processes.

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) acts as the universal energy currency of the cell. It consists of adenine, a ribose sugar, and three phosphate groups.

Energy from cell respiration
↓
ADP + Pᵢ ➔ ATP (energy stored in bond)
↓
ATP is hydrolyzed back to ADP + Pᵢ, releasing energy for cellular work
Application

ATP is used for various energy-requiring processes such as active transport across membranes, muscle contraction, and synthesizing macromolecules (e.g., DNA replication, protein synthesis).

Because the bond between the second and third phosphate groups is easily broken and reformed, it is perfectly suited for transferring energy quickly. Note that the amount of energy $\Delta G$ released during hydrolysis is roughly $-30.5 \text{ kJ/mol}$.

C1.2.2

Controlled Release of Energy

Key UnderstandingCell respiration is the controlled release of energy from organic compounds to produce ATP.

Glucose is the primary substrate for respiration, but lipids and proteins can also be used. The breakdown must be controlled; if it happened in one big step, most energy would be lost as heat (like burning).

Skill

Calculating the respiratory quotient (RQ) from the volume of carbon dioxide produced and the volume of oxygen consumed during respiration. $\text{RQ} = \frac{\text{CO}_2 \text{ produced}}{\text{O}_2 \text{ consumed}}$.

By breaking down glucose in a series of small, enzyme-catalyzed steps, the cell efficiently captures energy to form ATP.

C1.2.3

Anaerobic Respiration

Key UnderstandingAnaerobic cell respiration gives a small yield of ATP from glucose without the use of oxygen.

When oxygen is not available, cells can still produce a small amount of ATP (2 ATP per glucose) solely through glycolysis in the cytoplasm.

In Humans (Animals)

Glucose is converted into lactic acid (lactate).

  • Useful during intense exercise when oxygen supply is insufficient.
  • Reaction: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow 2 \text{ Lactic Acid}$

In Yeast (Fungi/Plants)

Glucose is converted into ethanol and carbon dioxide.

  • Basis for baking (CO₂ makes bread rise) and brewing.
  • Reaction: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow 2 \text{ Ethanol} + 2 \text{CO}_2$
C1.2.4

Aerobic Respiration

Key UnderstandingAerobic cell respiration requires oxygen and gives a large yield of ATP from glucose.

Aerobic respiration completely oxidizes glucose into carbon dioxide and water, releasing a much larger amount of energy (about 30-32 ATP per glucose).

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ ➔ 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Energy (ATP)
  • Takes place partially in the cytoplasm (glycolysis) but mostly in the mitochondria.
  • Mitochondria are perfectly adapted for this, with their double membrane, highly folded cristae, and matrix containing necessary enzymes.
C1.2.5 / C1.2.6 HL Only

Redox Reactions & Glycolysis

Key Understanding (HL) Cell respiration involves the oxidation and reduction of electron carriers. Phosphorylation makes molecules less stable.

In biological systems, oxidation is the loss of electrons (or loss of hydrogen), and reduction is the gain of electrons (or gain of hydrogen). The main electron carrier is NAD⁺, which becomes reduced to NADH + H⁺.

1. Glycolysis (Cytoplasm): Glucose is phosphorylated (requires 2 ATP) to make it less stable.
↓
2. Lysis & Oxidation: The 6C sugar splits into two 3C compounds, which are oxidized to form two pyruvate molecules.
↓
3. Link Reaction (Mitochondrial Matrix): Pyruvate is decarboxylated (loses CO₂) and oxidized (forms NADH). Acetyl-CoA is formed.
C1.2.7 HL Only

The Krebs Cycle

Key Understanding (HL) In the Krebs cycle, the oxidation of acetyl groups is coupled to the reduction of hydrogen carriers.

Acetyl-CoA (2C) combines with a 4C compound (oxaloacetate) to form a 6C compound (citrate). Through a series of decarboxylations and oxidations, the 4C compound is regenerated.

Krebs Cycle Yield (per Acetyl-CoA):
  • 2 CO₂ (waste product)
  • 3 NADH + H⁺ and 1 FADH₂ (reduced electron carriers)
  • 1 ATP (via substrate-level phosphorylation)
C1.2.8 HL Only

ETC and Chemiosmosis

Key Understanding (HL) Energy released by oxidation reactions is carried to the cristae of the mitochondria and used to pump protons.
  • Electron Transport: NADH and FADH₂ donate electrons to the chain in the inner mitochondrial membrane.
  • Proton Pumping: As electrons move down energy levels, complexes I, III, and IV pump H⁺ ions into the intermembrane space, creating a concentration gradient.
  • Chemiosmosis: Protons flow back into the matrix through ATP synthase, providing energy to phosphorylate ADP into ATP.
  • Final Acceptor: Oxygen is the final electron acceptor, combining with electrons and protons to form water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$). Without oxygen, the chain stops!
