B3.2.1 - B3.2.3

Blood Vessels: Arteries, Veins, and Capillaries

Key Understanding Arteries convey blood at high pressure from the ventricles to the tissues. Veins return blood to the atria. Capillaries exchange materials.

The mammalian transport system relies on three distinct types of blood vessels, each structurally adapted to its function.

Arteries

  • Transport blood away from the heart.
  • Thick muscular and elastic walls to withstand high pressure.
  • Narrow lumen helps maintain high pressure.

Veins

  • Transport blood towards the heart.
  • Thin walls, large lumen for low-pressure flow.
  • Contain valves to prevent backflow of blood.
Capillaries

Capillaries are the site of material exchange (e.g., O₂, CO₂, nutrients). Adaptations include:

  • Extremely narrow (around $10 \, \mu m$) allowing only one red blood cell to pass at a time.
  • Wall is only one cell thick (endothelium) minimizing diffusion distance.
  • Pores or fenestrations to allow tissue fluid to leak out.
B3.2.4 & B3.2.6

Pulse Rates and Coronary Heart Disease

Skill: B3.2.4 Measurement of Pulse Rates

The pulse can be felt in arteries (e.g., radial artery in the wrist or carotid artery in the neck) due to the elastic recoil of arterial walls after blood is pumped from the heart. Pulse rate is measured in beats per minute (bpm). You should be able to measure it accurately using fingers or digital sensors.

Application: B3.2.6 Occlusion of Coronary Arteries

Coronary arteries supply the heart muscle with O₂ and nutrients. Occlusion (blockage) can cause coronary heart disease (CHD).

  • Causes: Atherosclerosis (buildup of cholesterol/plaque), leading to blood clots (thrombosis).
  • Consequences: Lack of O₂ (ischemia) leads to anaerobic respiration, lactic acid buildup, and eventually muscle death (myocardial infarction or heart attack).
B3.2.7 - B3.2.8

Transpiration and Xylem Adaptations

Key Understanding Water is transported from roots to leaves during transpiration due to cohesion, tension, and adhesion.

The Transpiration Stream:

Water evaporates from spongy mesophyll and exits stomata
↑
Creates tension (negative pressure, $\Psi < 0$) pulling water up xylem
↑
Cohesion: Hydrogen bonds hold H₂O molecules together
↑
Adhesion: H₂O binds to hydrophilic xylem walls
Xylem Adaptations (B3.2.8)
  • Dead cells with no cytoplasm/organelles reduce resistance to flow.
  • Thickened with lignin (helical or ringed) to withstand tension and prevent collapse.
  • Pits in walls allow lateral movement of water.
B3.2.9 - B3.2.10

Tissue Distribution in Dicotyledonous Plants

Key Understanding Be able to identify the distribution of tissues in transverse sections of dicot stems and roots.

Dicot Stem

  • Vascular bundles are arranged in a ring near the outer edge.
  • Xylem is located on the inside, phloem on the outside.
  • Provides structural support to the stem.

Dicot Root

  • Vascular tissue is in a central cylinder (stele).
  • Xylem typically forms a central star shape.
  • Phloem is located between the arms of the xylem star.
  • Withstands pulling forces.
B3.2.11 - B3.2.14 HL Only

Tissue Fluid and Circulation Types

Tissue Fluid Formation (B3.2.11 - B3.2.13)
  • High hydrostatic pressure at the arteriole end forces plasma (minus large proteins) out of capillaries, forming tissue fluid.
  • At the venule end, lower hydrostatic pressure and higher solute concentration draw water back in via osmosis.
  • Excess fluid is drained by the lymphatic system.

Single Circulation (Fish)

  • Blood passes through the heart once per circuit.
  • Heart $\rightarrow$ Gills $\rightarrow$ Body $\rightarrow$ Heart.
  • Lower blood pressure for systemic circulation.

Double Circulation (Mammals)

  • Blood passes through the heart twice per circuit.
  • Pulmonary (lungs) and Systemic (body) circuits are separate.
  • Maintains high pressure for efficient O₂ delivery.
B3.2.15 - B3.2.16 HL Only

The Cardiac Cycle and Heart Adaptations

Key Understanding (HL) Adaptations of the heart and stages of the cardiac cycle (systole and diastole).
Skill: B3.2.16 Cardiac Cycle and ECG

Understand the roles of systole (contraction) and diastole (relaxation). An Electrocardiogram (ECG) records the electrical activity of the heart (P wave, QRS complex, T wave).

Atrial Systole: Atria contract, pushing blood into ventricles (AV valves open)
↓
Ventricular Systole: Ventricles contract, pressure rises, AV valves close (lub), semilunar valves open, blood ejected into arteries
↓
Diastole: Ventricles relax, pressure drops, semilunar valves close (dup), blood flows passively into atria and ventricles
B3.2.17 - B3.2.18 HL Only

Root Pressure and Phloem Translocation

B3.2.17 Root Pressure

Active transport of mineral ions into the root xylem lowers the water potential ($\Psi$). Water enters by osmosis, generating a positive pressure (root pressure) that pushes water up the stem.

Phloem Adaptations (B3.2.18)

Phloem transports organic compounds (like sucrose) via translocation.

  • Sieve Tube Elements: Lack a nucleus and have reduced organelles to maximize space for sap flow; sieve plates have pores to allow flow between cells.
  • Companion Cells: Contain many mitochondria to provide ATP for the active loading of sucrose into the sieve tubes.
