Habitat and Adaptation

Key UnderstandingOrganisms are adapted to the abiotic factors of their habitats.

A habitat is the specific place where a community, species, population, or organism lives, characterized by distinct physical conditions.

Application: Specific Adaptations

Organisms have specialized adaptations to survive in extreme abiotic conditions:

  • Sand Dunes (Marram Grass): Highly rolled leaves to trap moisture, thick waxy cuticle, and extensive root networks to prevent uprooting in loose sand.
  • Mangrove Swamps (Mangrove Trees): Aerial roots (pneumatophores) to obtain oxygen in waterlogged, anaerobic mud, and specialized glands to excrete excess salt (NaCl).

Abiotic Variables and Range of Tolerance

Key UnderstandingAbiotic variables affect species distribution and determine an organism's range of tolerance.

Every species has a range of tolerance for every abiotic factor (such as temperature, pH, or light intensity) within which it can survive.

Skill: Using Transect Data

Biologists use transect data and data loggers to correlate the distribution of an organism with abiotic variables. If an abiotic factor falls outside the tolerance range, the species cannot survive there. For a standard normal distribution of tolerance, most individuals are found within $\pm 1 \sigma$ (one standard deviation) of the optimum, and few survive near the extreme limits ($> \pm 2 \sigma$).

The distribution can often be modelled mathematically using standard bell-curve equations, where the optimum corresponds to the peak frequency.

B4.1.5

Conditions for Coral Reef Formation

Key UnderstandingSpecific abiotic conditions are required for coral reef formation.

Coral reefs are built by colonies of tiny animals called polyps that secrete calcium carbonate (CaCO₃). They have extremely narrow ranges of tolerance.

Abiotic FactorOptimal Range/Requirement
Water Depth Generally shallow (less than 50m) to allow sufficient light penetration for symbiotic zooxanthellae.
Temperature Optimal between $23^\circ \text{C}$ and $29^\circ \text{C}$. Too high causes coral bleaching.
Salinity High salinity (approx 35 ppt); corals cannot survive near river mouths where fresh water dilutes the ocean.
Clarity Clear water is essential. High turbidity (silt/sediment) blocks light and clogs coral polyps.
pH Slightly alkaline (pH $\approx$ 8.0 - 8.3) to allow precipitation of CaCO₃ skeletons. Ocean acidification (increasing CO₂) threatens this.

Biomes and Convergent Evolution

Key UnderstandingBiomes are groups of ecosystems with similar communities dictated by abiotic factors and convergent evolution.

The distribution of terrestrial biomes (e.g., tropical rainforest, hot desert, taiga) is primarily determined by two abiotic factors: temperature and precipitation.

Similar Abiotic Conditions across different continents
↓
Unrelated species face similar selective pressures
↓
Convergent Evolution: They evolve similar structural adaptations
Example: Cacti in the Americas and Euphorbias in Africa are completely unrelated plant families, but both evolved thick, fleshy stems and spines to survive in hot deserts.
B4.1.8

Adaptations in Specific Biomes

Key UnderstandingOrganisms show distinct adaptations to life in hot deserts and tropical rainforests.

Organisms in hot deserts (xerophytes) face severe water shortages, while those in tropical rainforests must compete intensely for light.

Hot Desert Adaptations

  • Xerophytes: Deep roots, reduced leaves (spines), thick waxy cuticles, CAM physiology to fix CO₂ at night.
  • Animals: Nocturnal behavior to avoid heat, water conservation mechanisms (e.g., concentrated urine).

Tropical Rainforest Adaptations

  • Plants: Broad leaves to capture light, drip tips to shed excess rain, buttress roots for stability in shallow soil, epiphytes climbing to reach the canopy.
  • Animals: Specialized diets, highly adapted for arboreal (tree-living) lifestyle.
