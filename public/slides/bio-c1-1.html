C1.1.1

Metabolism as the Web of Life

Key UnderstandingMetabolism is the web of all enzyme-catalysed reactions in a cell or organism.

Inside every single living cell, a vast, highly coordinated network of chemical reactions is constantly taking place. This complex web is collectively referred to as metabolism. Without enzymes acting as highly specific biological catalysts, these biochemical reactions would occur far too slowly to sustain life under normal physiological conditions.

Metabolism Hover to reveal definition
The totality of all enzyme-catalysed chemical reactions that occur within a living organism in order to maintain life, comprising both anabolic and catabolic pathways.

Metabolic pathways are broadly divided into two opposing but complementary categories:

  • Anabolism: The synthesis of complex molecules from simpler constituent monomers. This process is endergonic, meaning it requires an input of energy (usually in the form of ATP). A key example is protein synthesis via condensation reactions on ribosomes, or photosynthesis.
  • Catabolism: The breakdown of complex macromolecules into simpler components. This process is exergonic, meaning it releases energy, which can be captured to synthesize ATP. A primary example is cellular respiration (C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ATP) and digestion via hydrolysis reactions.
C1.1.2

Enzyme Specificity & The Induced-Fit Model

Key UnderstandingEnzyme specificities are explained by the complementary shape and chemical properties of the active site.

Enzymes are globular proteins that possess a highly specialized cleft or pocket on their surface known as the active site. Because of the unique folding of the polypeptide chain (tertiary structure), the active site has a very specific shape and distinct chemical properties (polar, non-polar, acidic, or basic amino acid residues). Consequently, only a specific substrate (or a small group of chemically similar substrates) can successfully bind to it.

Activation Energy ($E_A$) Hover to reveal definition
The initial energy input required to destabilize existing chemical bonds in the substrate(s) and initiate a chemical reaction. Enzymes function by significantly lowering this energy barrier.

While the older "Lock and Key" hypothesis suggested a rigid active site, modern biochemistry relies on the Induced-Fit Model. As the substrate enters the active site, it induces a slight conformational (shape) change in the enzyme. This moldable embrace grips the substrate more tightly, placing physical strain on its chemical bonds. This strain destabilizes the substrate, reducing the activation energy required to reach the transition state.

Step-by-step Catalysis:
  • Random Collision: The substrate and enzyme collide in an aqueous environment due to random thermal molecular motion (kinetic energy).
  • Binding: The substrate binds to the active site forming an Enzyme-Substrate Complex.
  • Induced Fit: The enzyme undergoes a conformational change to mold itself around the substrate, physically stressing its bonds.
  • Lowered $E_A$: The reaction proceeds rapidly, converting the substrate into product(s).
  • Release: Products are released, and the enzyme returns to its original conformation, ready to be reused.
C1.1.3

Environmental Effects on Enzyme Rate

Key UnderstandingTemperature, pH, and substrate concentration affect the rate of enzyme activity.

Because enzymes are proteins relying on fragile intra-molecular bonds (like hydrogen bonds and ionic interactions) to maintain their 3D shape, their activity is extremely sensitive to changes in their external environment. Extreme conditions can cause denaturation—a permanent, irreversible structural distortion of the active site that renders the enzyme completely inactive.

Temperature

  • As temperature rises, molecules gain kinetic energy, moving faster and colliding more frequently, increasing the reaction rate.
  • At the optimum temperature, the reaction rate is at its peak (e.g., ~37°C for human enzymes).
  • Above the optimum, increased thermal agitation breaks the delicate hydrogen bonds holding the protein structure together. The enzyme rapidly denatures, causing a steep plunge in activity.

pH Level

  • Enzymes possess an optimum pH (e.g., Pepsin at pH 2, Trypsin at pH 8) where their activity is maximized.
  • Deviating from this optimum alters the H+ or OH- ion concentrations in solution.
  • These ions interfere with the ionic bonds and charge distribution of the active site's amino acids, changing its shape and preventing substrate binding, leading to denaturation.

Substrate Concentration: As substrate concentration increases, the rate of reaction initially rises steeply because more frequent successful collisions occur between substrate and enzyme. However, the curve eventually plateaus (levels off). At this maximum rate ($V_{max}$), all active sites are continuously occupied; the enzymes are saturated, and adding more substrate cannot accelerate the reaction further.

Competitive vs Non-Competitive Inhibition

Key Understanding (HL) Enzyme inhibitors can be competitive or non-competitive, affecting kinetics differently.

Inhibitors are chemical substances that bind to an enzyme and significantly reduce its catalytic rate ($V_0$). They are vital in pharmacology and cellular regulation. Inhibitors are broadly categorized by the exact location they bind to the enzyme and how they interact with it.

A distinct receptor site on the enzyme situated far away from the active site. Binding of a molecule here induces a global conformational change in the enzyme.

C1.1.5 HL Only

Metabolic Pathways & Feedback Inhibition

Key Understanding (HL) Metabolic pathways consist of chains and cycles of enzyme-catalysed reactions, often regulated by end-product inhibition.

Metabolism rarely occurs in a single dramatic step. Instead, it proceeds via highly structured, multi-step sequences known as metabolic pathways. The product of one specific enzyme-catalyzed reaction immediately becomes the reactant (substrate) for the subsequent enzyme in the sequence. These pathways can form linear chains (e.g., glycolysis or the coagulation cascade) or complex repeating cycles (e.g., the Krebs Cycle or the Calvin Cycle).

Application: End-Product Inhibition (Negative Feedback) Cells must conserve energy and chemical resources. If a pathway continuously runs unchecked, the cell will over-produce a substance it does not immediately need. To prevent this, the final End Product of the pathway often acts as a non-competitive inhibitor for the very first enzyme in the chain (often called the allosteric or pacemaker enzyme).

Classic IB Example: In bacteria, the amino acid Threonine is converted into Isoleucine in a 5-step pathway. When Isoleucine accumulates to high levels, it binds to the allosteric site of Threonine deaminase, halting the entire pathway and preventing further unnecessary production of Isoleucine.
Review & Assessment

Check Your Understanding

Expand the sections below to test your grasp of the concepts covered in this module.

1. Distinguish between anabolism and catabolism.

Anabolism is the synthesis of complex molecules from simpler ones, requiring an input of energy (ATP) (e.g., protein synthesis). Catabolism is the breakdown of complex molecules into simpler ones, which releases energy (e.g., cellular respiration).

2. Explain how temperature affects enzyme activity.

As temperature increases, molecular kinetic energy increases, leading to more frequent collisions between enzymes and substrates, raising the reaction rate. At the optimum temperature, activity is highest. Above the optimum, thermal energy breaks the hydrogen bonds maintaining the enzyme's tertiary structure, causing denaturation and a rapid drop in activity.

3. HL Only: How does a competitive inhibitor affect $V_{max}$ and $K_m$?

A competitive inhibitor structurally mimics the substrate and binds to the active site. It does not change $V_{max}$, as its effects can be overcome by vastly increasing the substrate concentration. However, it increases the apparent $K_m$, meaning the enzyme's apparent affinity for the substrate decreases, requiring more substrate to reach half the maximal velocity.

4. Describe the mechanism of end-product inhibition.

In end-product inhibition, the final product of a metabolic pathway acts as a non-competitive inhibitor for an enzyme earlier in the pathway (often the first enzyme). It binds to an allosteric site, altering the shape of the active site. This stops the pathway when sufficient product has been synthesized, preventing the wastage of cellular resources.
