C1.1.1

Metabolism as the Web of Life

Key UnderstandingMetabolism is the web of all enzyme-catalysed reactions in a cell or organism.

Inside every cell, a vast network of chemical reactions is constantly taking place. This complex web is collectively referred to as metabolism. Without enzymes acting as biological catalysts, these reactions would occur too slowly to sustain life.

Substrate (Reactant) enters the active site
↓
Enzyme-Substrate Complex forms
↓
Catalysis occurs (Activation energy is lowered)
↓
Product(s) are released
  • Anabolism: Building up complex molecules from simpler ones (requires energy). Example: Protein synthesis.
  • Catabolism: Breaking down complex molecules into simpler ones (releases energy). Example: Cellular respiration (C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Energy).
C1.1.2

Enzyme Specificity & The Induced-Fit Model

Key UnderstandingEnzyme specificities are explained by the complementary shape and chemical properties of the active site.

Enzymes are globular proteins with a highly specific region called the active site. Only a specific substrate can bind to it. The modern understanding of this interaction is the Induced-Fit Model.

How Catalysis Happens:
  • Random Collision: The substrate and enzyme collide due to random molecular motion (kinetic energy).
  • Binding: The substrate binds to the active site.
  • Induced Fit: The enzyme undergoes a slight conformational change to mold itself around the substrate more tightly, stressing the substrate's bonds.
  • Lowered $E_A$: This physical stress lowers the activation energy ($E_A$) required for the reaction, rapidly converting substrate into product.
C1.1.3

Environmental Effects on Enzyme Rate

Key UnderstandingTemperature, pH, and substrate concentration affect the rate of enzyme activity.

Enzyme activity is highly sensitive to environmental conditions. Extreme changes can cause denaturation, a structural change in the enzyme's active site making it unable to bind substrate.

Temperature

  • Higher temperature increases kinetic energy, leading to more successful collisions.
  • Above the optimum temperature, hydrogen bonds break, denaturing the enzyme and dropping the reaction rate sharply.

pH Level

  • Each enzyme has an optimum pH.
  • Deviating from the optimum alters the active site's charge and shape, reducing activity or causing denaturation.
Data Analysis Skill: You should be able to interpret or sketch graphs of enzyme activity. A temperature graph is asymmetrical (gradual rise, sharp drop), while a pH graph is typically a symmetrical bell curve.

Competitive vs Non-Competitive Inhibition

Key Understanding (HL) Enzyme inhibitors can be competitive or non-competitive, affecting kinetics differently.

Inhibitors reduce the rate of enzymatic reactions ($V_0$). They are categorized by how they interact with the enzyme.

TypeBinding SiteEffect on $V_{max}$Effect on $K_m$ (Affinity)
Competitive Active Site (competes with substrate) Unchanged (can be overcome by adding more substrate) Increases (apparent affinity decreases)
Non-Competitive Allosteric Site (causes conformational change) Decreases (cannot be overcome by more substrate) Unchanged
C1.1.5 HL Only

Metabolic Pathways & Feedback Inhibition

Key Understanding (HL) Metabolic pathways consist of chains and cycles of enzyme-catalysed reactions, often regulated by end-product inhibition.

Metabolism doesn't happen in a single step. It involves multiple steps, where the product of one reaction becomes the substrate for the next. This can form a linear chain (like glycolysis) or a cycle (like the Krebs cycle).

Substrate → (Enzyme 1) → Intermediate A
↓
Intermediate A → (Enzyme 2) → Intermediate B
↓
Intermediate B → (Enzyme 3) → End Product
Application: End-Product Inhibition To prevent the cell from wasting resources, the final End Product often acts as a non-competitive inhibitor for Enzyme 1. When the end product accumulates, it binds to the allosteric site of Enzyme 1, halting the entire pathway. This is a classic example of negative feedback.
