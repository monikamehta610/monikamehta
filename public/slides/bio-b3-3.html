Adaptations for Movement

Key Understanding Movement is a universal feature of living organisms, with adaptations in both motile and sessile species.

Movement can be considered a defining characteristic of life. However, it takes very different forms depending on an organism's lifestyle.

Motile Organisms

Organisms capable of independent locomotion (moving from place to place).

  • Rely on musculoskeletal systems (muscles acting on skeletons).
  • Specialized adaptations for swimming, walking, flying.
  • Requires significant energy ($ATP$).

Sessile Organisms

Organisms that are fixed in one place (e.g., barnacles, corals, plants).

  • They still move! Think of plants growing towards light (phototropism).
  • Movement of internal fluids or tentacles for filter feeding.
  • Often have motile larval stages for dispersal.

The Sarcomere and Sliding Filament Model

Key Understanding Muscle contraction is explained by the sliding filament model, involving the interactions of actin and myosin within the sarcomere.

Skeletal muscles are composed of repeating functional units called sarcomeres, bordered by Z-lines. The contraction of a muscle occurs as these sarcomeres shorten.

Myosin heads bind to binding sites on the actin filaments (forming cross-bridges)
↓
Using $ATP$ hydrolysis, the myosin heads pivot (the "power stroke")
↓
Actin filaments are pulled towards the M-line (center of the sarcomere)
↓
The sarcomere shortens, but the filaments themselves do not change length!

Titin, Relaxation, and Antagonistic Muscles

B3.3.3 The role of the protein titin in elastic recoil, and the necessity of antagonistic muscles for relaxation.

Muscles can only pull (contract); they cannot actively push themselves back to their original length. Therefore, muscles must work in antagonistic pairs.

Application (B3.3.8)

The internal and external intercostal muscles are an antagonistic pair crucial for ventilation. When the external intercostals contract, they pull the ribs up and out (inspiration). The internal intercostals contract to pull the ribs down and in (forced expiration).

The Role of Titin
  • Titin is a massive, spring-like protein in the sarcomere (connecting the Z-line to the M-line).
  • It prevents overstretching of the muscle and provides elastic recoil when the muscle relaxes.
  • When an antagonistic muscle pulls the original muscle back to its starting length, titin helps restore the sarcomere's resting structure.

Motor Units and Neuromuscular Junctions

Key Understanding The structure and function of motor units, including motor neurons, muscle fibers, and neuromuscular junctions.

A single motor neuron does not just connect to one muscle fiber; it branches to innervate multiple fibers. This entire complex is called a motor unit.

Action potential arrives at the end of a motor neuron
↓
Neurotransmitter (Acetylcholine, $ACh$) is released across the neuromuscular junction
↓
Receptors on the muscle fiber membrane (sarcolemma) trigger depolarization
↓
Calcium ions ($Ca^{2+}$) are released, initiating the sliding filament mechanism
Exam Tip: Fine motor control (like in your fingers) relies on small motor units (1 neuron to ~10 fibers), while powerful movements (like in your thigh) use large motor units (1 neuron to ~1000 fibers).

Skeletons and Synovial Joints

B3.3.5 Skeletons provide anchorage points for muscles and act as levers.
B3.3.6 Movement at synovial joints (e.g., the human hip).

Skeletons (whether endoskeletons in vertebrates or exoskeletons in arthropods) provide rigid anchorage points. When muscles contract, they pull on bones via tendons, acting as levers to produce movement.

ComponentFunction in Synovial Joints
Bones Provide a rigid framework and act as levers.
Cartilage Covers the ends of bones to reduce friction and absorb shock.
Synovial Fluid Lubricates the joint, further reducing friction and supplying nutrients to cartilage.
Ligaments Connect bone to bone, providing stability and restricting excessive movement.
Tendons Connect muscle to bone, transmitting the force of contraction.

Skills, Ecology, and Adaptations

Skill (B3.3.7): Joint Range of Motion

Biologists and physiotherapists use tools like goniometers or computer-assisted motion analysis to quantify the range of motion of joints. For example, comparing the multi-axial movement of the hip (a ball-and-socket joint) to the uni-axial movement of the knee (a hinge joint).

B3.3.9 Reasons for locomotion in animals.

Animals expend massive amounts of energy ($ATP$) to move. The evolutionary benefits must outweigh the costs: finding food, escaping predators, finding mates, and dispersing to new habitats.

Application (B3.3.10): Marine Mammals

Marine mammals (like whales or seals) exhibit striking adaptations for swimming. This includes a streamlined body shape to reduce water viscosity drag (as seen in A1.1), modified limbs (flippers), and specialized ventilation behaviors to minimize surfacing time.
