Populations and Sampling

Key Understanding Populations are interacting groups of organisms of the same species. Their size is estimated using random sampling.

A population is defined as a group of organisms of the same species living in the same area at the same time. Since counting every individual is usually impossible, ecologists use sampling techniques.

Skill

Using random quadrat sampling to estimate population size for sessile (non-moving) organisms.

Place a quadrat randomly in an area
↓
Count the number of individuals of a species within the quadrat
↓
Repeat multiple times and calculate the average per quadrat
↓
Multiply by the total area to estimate total population size
C4.1.4

The Lincoln Index (Capture-Mark-Release-Recapture)

Key Understanding The Lincoln index is used to estimate the population size of motile organisms.

For animals that move, quadrats are ineffective. Instead, a portion of the population is captured, marked, and released. Later, another sample is captured, and the number of marked individuals is recorded.

Skill

Calculate population size using the Lincoln Index formula.

$$N = \frac{n_1 \times n_2}{n_3}$$

  • N: Total population estimate
  • $n_1$: Number caught and marked in first sample
  • $n_2$: Total number caught in second sample
  • $n_3$: Number of marked individuals caught in second sample
C4.1.5 & C4.1.7

Population Growth Curves

Key Understanding Populations exhibit exponential and sigmoidal growth curves, controlled by carrying capacity and resources.

Populations tend to grow exponentially until they reach the carrying capacity ($K$) of their environment, at which point density-dependent factors slow the growth, creating a sigmoid (S-shaped) curve.

Application

Modeling sigmoid population growth curves.

Communities and Interspecific Relationships

Key Understanding A community consists of all the interacting organisms within an ecosystem.

Species within a community interact in various ways. These interspecific interactions shape community structure and dynamics.

InteractionSpecies 1Species 2Example
Mutualism + (Benefits) + (Benefits) Bees and flowers
Herbivory + (Benefits) - (Harmed) Caterpillar eating leaves
Predation + (Benefits) - (Harmed) Lion hunting zebra
Parasitism + (Benefits) - (Harmed) Tick feeding on a dog
Competition - (Harmed) - (Harmed) Two species of barnacles competing for space
C4.1.16

Predator-Prey Relationships

Key Understanding Predator–prey relationships serve as a key example of density-dependent population control.

Predator and prey populations often fluctuate in coupled cycles. The predator cycle typically lags slightly behind the prey cycle.

Prey population increases (abundant food for predators)
↓
Predator population increases due to more food
↓
High predation causes prey population to decrease
↓
Less food causes predator population to decrease, allowing prey to recover
C4.1.17 HL Only

Top-Down and Bottom-Up Control

Key Understanding (HL) Community populations are regulated by either top-down or bottom-up control mechanisms.

Top-Down Control

Population size is regulated by predation at higher trophic levels.

  • Predators control herbivore populations.
  • Example: Wolves in Yellowstone controlling elk populations.

Bottom-Up Control

Population size is regulated by the availability of resources at lower trophic levels.

  • Nutrients and producers limit herbivores.
  • Example: Algal blooms driven by nutrient availability, dictating zooplankton levels.
C4.1.18 HL Only

Allelopathy and Antibiotic Secretion

Key Understanding (HL) Allelopathy and the secretion of antibiotics are processes used to deter potential competitors by releasing chemical substances.

Organisms can engage in chemical warfare to reduce interspecific competition.

  • Allelopathy: Plants release specific chemicals (allelochemicals) into the soil to inhibit the growth or germination of competing plant species. Example: The Black Walnut tree ($Juglans$ $nigra$) secretes juglone.
  • Antibiotic Secretion: Microorganisms (like fungi and bacteria) secrete antibiotics to kill or inhibit competing microbes. Example: $Penicillium$ fungus secretes penicillin to kill bacteria.
