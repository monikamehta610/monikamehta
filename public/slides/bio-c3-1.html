C3.1.1

Nervous vs Endocrine Coordination

Key UnderstandingThe integration of body systems is achieved through the coordinated actions of the nervous system and the endocrine system.

To maintain a stable internal environment (homeostasis) and respond to stimuli, multicellular organisms require rapid and sustained communication between different parts of the body.

Nervous System

  • Signal: Electrical impulses (action potentials) and chemical neurotransmitters.
  • Transmission: Along neurons (nerve fibers).
  • Speed: Extremely rapid (milliseconds).
  • Duration: Short-lived response.
  • Target: Specific cells (muscles, glands).

Endocrine System

  • Signal: Chemical hormones.
  • Transmission: Through the bloodstream.
  • Speed: Slower (seconds to days).
  • Duration: Long-lasting response.
  • Target: Many target organs/cells with specific receptors.
C3.1.2

Homeostasis & Negative Feedback

Key UnderstandingHomeostasis involves maintaining the internal environment between limits, primarily via negative feedback loops.

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment in an organism despite changes in the external environment. This includes regulating parameters such as core temperature, blood pH (maintained closely at pH $7.35 - 7.45$), and blood glucose concentration.

1. Stimulus: Change in the internal environment (e.g., body temperature rises).
↓
2. Sensor/Receptor: Detects the change and sends a signal.
↓
3. Control Centre: Processes the signal and formulates a response (e.g., hypothalamus).
↓
4. Effector: Organ or tissue that carries out the response (e.g., sweat glands).
↓
5. Response: The change is counteracted, bringing the parameter back to the set point.
SkillDrawing a negative feedback loop diagram. Ensure the cycle clearly shows how the response inhibits the original stimulus.
C3.1.3

Blood Glucose Regulation

Key UnderstandingThe control of blood glucose concentration involves insulin and glucagon, secreted by $\alpha$ and $\beta$ cells in the pancreatic islets.
When Blood Glucose is HIGH (e.g., after eating):
  • $\beta$ (beta) cells in the pancreas detect high glucose and secrete insulin.
  • Insulin stimulates muscle and liver cells to take up glucose and convert it into glycogen ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow$ Glycogen).
  • Blood glucose levels decrease back to the set point.
When Blood Glucose is LOW (e.g., fasting/exercise):
  • $\alpha$ (alpha) cells in the pancreas detect low glucose and secrete glucagon.
  • Glucagon stimulates liver cells to break down glycogen into glucose and release it into the blood.
  • Blood glucose levels increase back to the set point.
C3.1.4

Diabetes Mellitus

Key UnderstandingThe causes and consequences of Type I and Type II diabetes differ significantly.
FeatureType I DiabetesType II Diabetes
Onset Usually during childhood ("Early-onset"). Usually during adulthood ("Late-onset").
Cause Autoimmune destruction of $\beta$ cells in the pancreas. Body cannot produce insulin. Target cells become insensitive (resistant) to insulin. Down-regulation of insulin receptors.
Risk Factors Genetics. Obesity, poor diet, lack of exercise, age, genetics.
Treatment Regular insulin injections. Dietary modifications, exercise, medication, and sometimes insulin injections.
ApplicationEvaluating the risk factors for Type II diabetes. Consider epidemiological data showing the correlation between sugar-rich diets, sedentary lifestyles, and the rising global incidence of Type II diabetes.

The Hypothalamus & Pituitary Gland

Key Understanding (HL) The hypothalamus and pituitary gland link the nervous and endocrine systems.

The hypothalamus is a region of the brain that serves as the main control center for homeostasis. It receives information from nerves throughout the body and coordinates endocrine responses by controlling the pituitary gland.

Hypothalamus (Nervous tissue)
↓
Secretes neurohormones (e.g., TRH, CRH) or sends direct nerve signals
↓
Pituitary Gland (Anterior & Posterior Lobes)
↓
Secretes various hormones (e.g., ADH, Oxytocin, TSH, FSH) into the blood
↓
Target Organs (e.g., Kidneys, Uterus, Thyroid, Gonads)
C3.1.6 HL Only

Autonomic Control of Heart Rate

Key Understanding (HL) The autonomic nervous system controls involuntary processes such as heart rate.

The cardiac muscle is myogenic (it can contract on its own), but the Sinoatrial (SA) node (the pacemaker) is regulated by the autonomic nervous system located in the medulla oblongata.

Sympathetic Nervous System

The "Fight or Flight" response.

  • Releases noradrenaline (norepinephrine) at the SA node.
  • Effect: Increases heart rate.
  • Stimulated by: Exercise, stress, low blood pH (high CO$_2$).

Parasympathetic Nervous System

The "Rest and Digest" response.

  • Signals via the vagus nerve, releasing acetylcholine at the SA node.
  • Effect: Decreases heart rate.
  • Stimulated by: Relaxation, normal resting conditions.
Exam Tip (HL): Remember that adrenaline (epinephrine) from the adrenal glands also increases heart rate, demonstrating how both the nervous and endocrine systems integrate to control a single response.
