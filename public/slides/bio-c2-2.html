C2.2.1

Neurons & The Nervous System

Key UnderstandingNeurons transmit electrical impulses.

The nervous system allows organisms to interact with their environment by transmitting signals rapidly across the body. The fundamental unit of this system is the neuron.

  • Dendrites: Short-branched nerve fibres that convert chemical information from other neurons or receptor cells into electrical signals.
  • Soma (Cell Body): Contains the nucleus and most organelles, where metabolic processes occur.
  • Axon: An elongated nerve fibre that transmits electrical signals (action potentials) to terminal regions for communication with other neurons or effectors.

C2.2.2

The Resting Potential

Key UnderstandingNeurons pump sodium and potassium ions across their membranes to generate a resting potential.

When a neuron is not transmitting a signal, it has a resting potential of about $-70\text{ mV}$. The inside of the neuron is negative relative to the outside.

How is it maintained?

The sodium-potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺ pump) uses ATP to actively transport ions:

  • Expels 3 Na⁺ ions out of the cell.
  • Brings 2 K⁺ ions into the cell.
  • This creates an electrochemical gradient, with more Na⁺ outside and more K⁺ inside, maintaining a net negative charge inside.
C2.2.3

Action Potentials

Key UnderstandingAn action potential consists of depolarization and repolarization of the neuron.

An action potential is a rapid change in membrane potential, consisting of two main phases:

1. Depolarization

Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open. Na⁺ rushes into the cell down its concentration gradient. Membrane potential changes from $-70\text{ mV}$ to $+30\text{ mV}$.

2. Repolarization

Na⁺ channels close. Voltage-gated K⁺ channels open. K⁺ rushes out of the cell. Membrane potential drops back below resting potential (hyperpolarization) before resetting.

Skill

Analysis of oscilloscope traces showing resting potentials and action potentials.

C2.2.4

Propagation of Action Potentials

Key UnderstandingNerve impulses are action potentials propagated along the axons of neurons.

An action potential in one part of the axon triggers an action potential in the next part. This is called propagation.

  • The influx of Na⁺ during depolarization diffuses inside the axon, triggering adjacent voltage-gated Na⁺ channels to open.
  • This results in a wave of depolarization moving along the axon in one direction.
  • Impulses only travel in one direction because the recently depolarized area enters a refractory period where channels are temporarily inactive.
Application

Understanding threshold potentials. An action potential is only initiated if the threshold potential (approx. $-55\text{ mV}$) is reached. It is an "all-or-nothing" response.

C2.2.5 HL Only

Myelination & Saltatory Conduction

Key Understanding (HL) Myelination of nerve fibres allows for saltatory conduction.

Some axons are coated with a myelin sheath (formed by Schwann cells), which acts as an insulating layer.

  • Nodes of Ranvier: The myelin sheath is punctuated by small gaps called nodes of Ranvier.
  • Saltatory Conduction: The action potential "jumps" from node to node, because voltage-gated ion channels are only concentrated at the nodes.
  • This makes impulse transmission significantly faster (up to $100\text{ m/s}$) compared to unmyelinated axons (approx. $1\text{ m/s}$).
C2.2.6

Synapses

Key UnderstandingSynapses are junctions between neurons, and between neurons and receptor or effector cells.

Since electrical impulses cannot cross the physical gap (synaptic cleft) between cells, the signal must be converted into a chemical message called a neurotransmitter.

C2.2.7

Synaptic Transmission

Key UnderstandingWhen presynaptic neurons are depolarized they release a neurotransmitter into the synapse.

Once neurotransmitters bind to the postsynaptic receptors, they cause ion channels to open, which can either excite or inhibit the postsynaptic neuron.

Excitatory (EPSP)

Causes opening of Na⁺ channels, leading to depolarization. Increases the chance of firing an action potential.

Inhibitory (IPSP)

Causes opening of K⁺ or Cl⁻ channels, leading to hyperpolarization. Decreases the chance of firing an action potential.

Application

Secretion and reabsorption of acetylcholine by neurons at synapses. Neonicotinoid pesticides block synaptic transmission in insects by binding to acetylcholine receptors.
